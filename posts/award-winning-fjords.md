I have spent most of my career designing coastlines for custom planets. I am particularly proud of the fjords of Norway. The judges were too, though they were suspicious of the crinkly edges.
<!-- /excerpt -->

[[TOC]]

Fjords are, by general consensus, the pinnacle of coastline design. They are dramatic, impractical, and excellent at making boats feel small. A well-designed fjord should look like a glacier has changed its mind several times and then given up.

## Design Principles

1. Start with a solid mountain. Do not skimp on altitude; a low fjord is merely a damp ditch.
2. Apply pressure from a large, patient glacier. Impatience produces canals, which are a different department.
3. Allow the sea to enter, preferably with dramatic lighting and a sense of foreboding.
4. Add waterfalls at irregular intervals. A fjord without waterfalls is a hallway with a leak.

> I won an award for the Norwegian fjords. The judges said they were 'pointless but beautiful.' I took it as a compliment.

## The Importance of Crinkliness

The best fjords are not straight. A straight fjord is a river, and rivers are designed by the rainfall team, who have much less imagination. A proper fjord should zigzag, double back, and occasionally surprise you with a small village built by people who clearly had no better options.

## Common Mistakes

Amateur designers often make fjords too warm. Fjords are supposed to be cold. A warm fjord is a lake, and a lake is a completely different design problem.[^1]

[^1]: I was once asked to design a warm fjord. I refused. The client then asked for a fjord with a retractable roof. I retired to a small moon for several weeks.

## Conclusion

If you are ever designing a coastline, include fjords. They are difficult to clean, impossible to navigate, and absolutely worth the effort.
